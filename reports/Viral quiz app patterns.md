# Make the hawker quiz share itself

A no-backend Singaporean-food personality quiz should run the engine that produced tens of millions of quiz takes in the early 2010s — the **results-first, plurality-scored "which ___ are you" bucket model** — and wrap it in the one growth mechanic that needs no server at all: a **Wordle-style spoiler-free share artifact**. The format's peak is well documented: BuzzFeed's **"What State Do You Actually Belong In?" drew 48,834,284 takes**, and food already proved itself as a top identity category with **"What Food Matches Your Personality?" at 9,877,592 takes** ([BuzzFeed](https://www.buzzfeed.com/andrewziegler/best-buzzfeed-quizzes)). Food-and-place identity is exactly the flattering, tractable archetype that spreads, and Singaporean hawker dishes supply a ready-made cast of six to eight distinct, locally legible characters. Pair that cast with **5–10 questions, a "Question N of M" counter, 48px thumb targets, and URL-encoded results**, and a single static page completes at **65–85%** while generating a shareable image with no backend ([Outgrow](https://outgrow.co/blog/quiz-engagement-benchmarks-completion-rates)). The binding constraint is not the quiz itself but distribution: the mechanics still work, yet the free social reach that once carried them has collapsed ([Wikipedia: Feed (Facebook)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feed_(Facebook))). The rest of this report turns those findings into concrete build decisions.

## Results-first plurality is the engine food was born to run

The dominant viral format is the "which ___ are you" identity quiz, and its mechanics are simpler than they look. BuzzFeed's own build process, described by managing editorial director Summer Anne Burton, was to **"always write the results first,"** assign each answer a personality, and let the backend behave like **"a hidden version of a classic Cosmo quiz. You have six buckets, and whichever answer you have the most in the bucket, that's the result that you get"** ([HuffPost](https://www.huffpost.com/entry/buzzfeed-quiz-how-do-they-work_n_4810992)). That plurality method — tag each answer with a result id, tally, return the maximum — is trivial to implement, resistant to gaming, and guarantees the user lands in one of a small set of well-written identities. The format was authored for mass reproduction: BuzzFeed published **7,350 quizzes in 2014 alone, generating 1.2 billion hits**, an average of **163,265 views per quiz**, and built reusable visual templates of **"little square questions that have text on them and look pretty"** so non-designers could mass-produce them ([Interhacktives](https://www.interhacktives.com/2015/01/18/buzzfeeds-success-means-2015/); [HuffPost](https://www.huffpost.com/entry/buzzfeed-quiz-how-do-they-work_n_4810992)). The over-performing categories in BuzzFeed's decade retrospective are place, animal, fandom, and food — which is precisely why a hawker-dish quiz, sitting at the intersection of food and place, is a favorable raw material ([BuzzFeed](https://www.buzzfeed.com/andrewziegler/best-buzzfeed-quizzes)).

The alternatives matter for the one design choice that will define the app. The documented casual-engine default is the most-picked-outcome scorer with **ties broken randomly**, while a numeric variant sums per-answer points into multiple categories to show a **breakdown rather than a single winner** ([Gravity Forms Personality Quiz Add-On](https://github.com/dabernathy89/gravity-forms-personality-quiz-add-on)). The React clone of the BuzzFeed format binds every answer to a **`resultID`** and exposes the result screen, restart, and share hooks as first-class parts of the component, confirming that the share flow is engine, not add-on ([react-buzzfeed-quiz](https://github.com/amamenko/react-buzzfeed-quiz)). If the app instead wants a scored "how compatible are you with X" result, the explicit model is OkCupid's **answer + acceptable answers + importance weight** triple ([Wikipedia: OkCupid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OkCupid)). Psychometric instruments confirm the same handful of patterns at higher rigor — Likert items summed and reverse-scored into continuous traits ([Wikipedia: Big Five](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Five_personality_traits)), and outcome assignment by a **midpoint cut-off** that can flip two nearly identical people into opposite categories ([Wikipedia: MBTI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myers%E2%80%93Briggs_Type_Indicator)). That instability is measured, not hypothetical: **between 39% and 76% of respondents get a different MBTI type when retaking it after only five weeks** ([Wikipedia: MBTI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myers%E2%80%93Briggs_Type_Indicator)). A hard single-label food result carries the same retake-flip risk, so the recommendation is a **plurality winner shown alongside a top-three percentage breakdown**, with the ambiguity handled by a **disclosed tie-breaker question** rather than a silent coin flip — the Pottermore Sorting Hat does exactly this, sampling **a maximum of 7 questions from a pool of 27, or 8 if it "can not decide between houses and asks your opinion"** ([Harry Potter Wiki: Sorting Hat](https://harrypotter.fandom.com/wiki/Sorting_Hat)). This converts a tie from a bug into an intentional, re-shareable moment.

## Flattering, hedge-worded copy does more sharing work than accuracy

Quiz results spread because they supply identity confirmation, and the psychology behind that is the strongest-evidenced part of this entire research set. The **Barnum (Forer) effect** shows that people rate vague, general personality descriptions as highly accurate: in Forer's classic 1949 demonstration, **39 students each received the identical 13-item vignette and rated its accuracy 4.30 out of 5** ([Wikipedia: Barnum effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnum_effect)). Accuracy ratings rise when the subject **believes the analysis is personalized**, trusts the evaluator, and when the analysis **lists mainly positive traits**, with "at times" hedging doing much of the work ([Wikipedia: Barnum effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnum_effect)). Burton's own account matches the science: her most successful quizzes were built so **"the results feel personal and that you can relate to them… It's not scientific, but if you have a good attitude, that doesn't keep it from being fun,"** and she compared quiz results to astrology rather than to Myers-Briggs ([HuffPost](https://www.huffpost.com/entry/buzzfeed-quiz-how-do-they-work_n_4810992)). The share loop is therefore: low-effort image-tap input → a psychologically "personal" output → a self-expressive badge → a post to a social graph → a friend repeats it. Fandom quizzes rode a pre-existing internal game — **"everybody plays that game, in their heads or with their friends already. So they want to see what an objective source is going to tell them"** ([HuffPost](https://www.huffpost.com/entry/buzzfeed-quiz-how-do-they-work_n_4810992)) — which is why a familiar hawker-dish cast ("are you laksa or char kway teow?") is an advantage, not a limitation.

Two cautions are load-bearing. First, controversy is a secondary accelerant rather than the core engine: the biggest-take BuzzFeed quizzes were flattering identity categories, while charged framings such as **"How Privileged Are You?" (16.9M takes)** and **"How Sexually Pure Are You?" (3.0M takes)** broke through because they invited argument ([BuzzFeed](https://www.buzzfeed.com/andrewziegler/best-buzzfeed-quizzes)). A food quiz should pick the warm, tribal version of identity rather than manufactured outrage. Second, the same mechanics have a documented dark side: BuzzFeed **recorded quiz answers with unique IDs**, and an analytics expert warned that aggregate answers to a sensitive quiz meant roughly **2 million people had disclosed personal facts** ([Newsweek](https://www.newsweek.com/does-buzzfeed-know-which-backstreet-boy-you-should-actually-marry-256280)). A no-backend app sidesteps most of that risk by never transmitting answers — but only if it is actually backend-free, so the result must be computed and encoded **entirely on the client**. The practical copywriting rule for the Singaporean food quiz: write each of the six to eight result archetypes first, make every one of them flattering and specific enough to feel earned, and never imply health or clinical validity — the Forer effect rewards warmth and vagueness, and punishes overclaiming.

## Wordle's spoiler-free grid is the strongest no-server growth lever

Of every mechanic reviewed, the shareable result artifact has the highest leverage per unit of engineering, and Wordle is the clean proof. Wordle grew from **90 players on 1 November 2021 to more than 300,000 by 2 January 2022 and over 2 million a week later**, with **1.2 million results shared on Twitter between 1 and 13 January 2022** — driven by an **emoji grid that reveals no answer**, borrowed from a friend group's homemade version ([Wikipedia: Wordle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wordle)). The format deliberately caps sessions at about **three minutes a day** and seeds one puzzle per day for everyone, creating scarcity that leaves players wanting more ([Wikipedia: Wordle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wordle)). NYT bought it for a price in the **"low seven figures"** five months after that viral burst and attributes **"tens of millions" of new puzzle users** to it; by 2024, NYT Games logged **11.1 billion puzzles played**, with **over 10 million daily players and over one million premium subscribers** ([NYT](https://www.nytimes.com/2022/01/31/crosswords/nyt-wordle-purchase.html); [The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/2025/6/10/24174642/nyt-games-crossplay-connections-wordle)). For a personality quiz, the equivalent artifact is the result card, and the recap industry shows how to package it: Spotify Wrapped 2024 shipped a dedicated **"Summary Share Card"** plus per-story assets in **16x9, 4x5, 9x16, and 4x6** ratios rather than one universal image, across **184 markets** ([Spotify Newsroom](https://newsroom.spotify.com/2024-12-04/10-years-spotify-wrapped/)).

Link previews are the other half of the artifact. Open Graph pages are driven by four required properties — **`og:title`, `og:type`, `og:image`, `og:url`** — plus a recommended description, and `og:image` should declare **width, height, and alt** ([The Open Graph Protocol](https://ogp.me/)). Facebook guidance puts the image at **1200×630 (1.91:1), under 5 MB, with a title capped around 70 characters**, and X's large-image card accepts an image as small as 280×150 px under 1 MB with a description capped near 200 characters ([CSS-Tricks](https://css-tricks.com/essential-meta-tags-social-media/)). The hard constraint is that **dynamic per-result OG images normally require a backend** — Vercel's `@vercel/og` renders 1200×630 cards from Satori + Resvg but runs on the **Node.js runtime** ([Vercel OG Image Generation](https://vercel.com/docs/og-image-generation)). The backend-free resolution is to **pre-generate one static OG image per food archetype at build time** and reference it from `/results/<code>.png`, so the runtime stays a pure static page while every shared result still unfurls with an on-brand card. Client-side, the downloadable card should be rendered in the browser with **html-to-image — 5.2 kB gzipped, zero dependencies, explicitly tested on Safari 16** — then handed to the OS via the Web Share API, falling back to an `<a download>` click; that is dramatically lighter than **html2canvas (46.5 kB gzipped), whose own README calls it "very experimental… don't recommend using it in a production environment,"** and far simpler than DOM-serialization approaches such as dom-to-image, which **does not support Safari** ([Bundlephobia](https://bundlephobia.com); [html-to-image](https://github.com/bubkoo/html-to-image); [html2canvas](https://github.com/niklasvh/html2canvas); [dom-to-image](https://github.com/tsayen/dom-to-image)). Keep the card to **one idea and one hero visual**, with a fixed logo lockup and a generous central safe area, because the 4:5 and 9:16 crops are decided upstream by platforms that will crop the card for feed and Stories placements.

## Six questions, not sixty: completion benchmarks set the quiz shape

Question count is the most over-theorized variable, and the two best benchmark sets disagree in shape. Outgrow's quiz-focused marketing data finds a monotonic decline and names **seven questions as the sweet spot**, with **3–7 questions completing at 65–85%, 8–15 at 45–65%, and 16+ at 25–45%**; it puts **personality quizzes specifically at 60–80% completion**, identifies **question 3 as the danger zone**, and targets **2–4 minutes of total engagement** ([Outgrow](https://outgrow.co/blog/quiz-engagement-benchmarks-completion-rates)). Survicate's much larger platform study (**n = 21,863**, 2025) instead finds a **U-shaped curve**: 1 question completes at **85.7%**, 5 questions at **72.5%**, the 21–40 bucket bottoms out at **70.5%**, and only very long 101–150-question surveys rebound to **83.0%** — a region Survicate itself attributes to **self-selection and small sample size**, explicitly stating it is **"not saying longer surveys are generally preferable"** and labeling 6–60 questions the **"Valley of Death"** ([Survicate](https://survicate.com/blog/how-many-questions-should-surveys-have/)). The defensible planning target for a fun food quiz is therefore **5–10 questions**, which sits in the good band of both curves and stays under Outgrow's 2–4 minute window. Design explicitly for the Q3 cliff by front-loading the most curiosity-driving question — for this app, a vivid image-choice like "pick your perfect breakfast" — and never place a form field or email gate mid-quiz, which Outgrow says **"kills momentum"** ([Outgrow](https://outgrow.co/blog/quiz-engagement-benchmarks-completion-rates)).

Progress feedback is the one completion lever with a quantified, repeated claim, and the supporting research is stronger than the vendor number. Outgrow claims **visual progress bars increase completion by 12–18%**, a figure with no published methodology, but classic HCI research found people shown a **continuous moving progress bar were willing to wait about 3× longer** than a control group and reported higher satisfaction ([Outgrow](https://outgrow.co/blog/quiz-engagement-benchmarks-completion-rates); [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progress-indicators/)). NN/g's concrete rules are that feedback should be immediate, a **looped indicator suits 2–9 second waits**, and a **percent-done indicator suits waits of 10 seconds or more**; notably, for surveys, **progress indicators that start fast and end slow reduce drop-off** — the opposite of the usual wait-animation advice ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progress-indicators/)). Since each quiz step takes seconds, the right pattern is a **persistent "Question N of M" step counter**, not a bare percentage, and early progress should be weighted to feel fast. Mobile is the default surface: Outgrow puts **60% of quiz traffic on mobile**, so answers need **≥48px tap targets with ≥8px spacing** ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/accessible-tap-targets)), and Steven Hoober's 1,333-observation study found **49% of phone use is one-handed, 36% cradled, and 15% two-handed**, with two-handed use **90% portrait** — putting the primary "See result" CTA in the **bottom third** and out of the top corners that require regripping ([UXmatters](https://www.uxmatters.com/mt/archives/2013/02/how-do-users-really-hold-mobile-devices.php)). Performance must meet Google's **Core Web Vitals at the 75th percentile — LCP ≤2.5s, INP ≤200ms, CLS ≤0.1** — because a quiz lives entirely on the tap response ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/vitals)).

## A static page can borrow the loops — but not the crowd

The non-personality quiz genres clarify exactly which engagement mechanics survive a backend-free build. The strongest transferable finding is Wordle's **spoiler-free share artifact**, which turns a private three-minute activity into free social distribution with zero server involvement, and the deterministic daily drop — seeding the question index from the calendar date so everyone "gets today's" — faithfully reproduces scarcity inside a hardcoded content bank ([Wikipedia: Wordle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wordle)). Duolingo supplies the habit evidence: making the streak easier (one lesson, decoupled from the daily goal) produced **+3.3% Day-14 retention and +1% DAU**, and learners who reach a **7-day streak are 2.4× more likely to return the next day**, with the share of daily learners on a 7+ day streak rising **from about a third to just over half within a year** ([Duolingo Blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak/)). A **localStorage streak, personal best, and games-played counter** are the no-account version of that loop; QuizUp's fixed **160-point ceiling with −1 point per second and a minimum of 11** is the model for a legible "beat your best / beat par" target, and its self-reported **40 minutes per day** and **1 billion matches in 197 countries by March 2014** show how potent speed-plus-score is ([Wikipedia: QuizUp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QuizUp)). HQ Trivia's **10-second window, elimination, and extra-life tokens** are the same texture without the live infrastructure or prize liability — and the collapse of HQ after its **record 2.38 million concurrent players on 28 March 2018** is the reminder that the format and the business model are separable ([Wikipedia: HQ (video game)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HQ_(video_game))). Even the social-occasion model translates in hotseat form: Jackbox's **room-code, phone-as-controller, no-account** design proves a quiz can be multiplayer with zero installs, and its **Fibbage/Quiplash** pattern of user-supplied answers can be faked locally ([Wikipedia: Jackbox Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackbox_Games)).

State management is where the no-backend constraint becomes a design decision rather than a limitation. A finished quiz should encode its **result id in the URL** (for example `?r=laksa`) so the same static page re-renders a personalized result for anyone who opens the link; URL parameters are the shareable source of truth because recipients never share the sender's localStorage. In-progress answers can be resumed from **localStorage, which is origin-scoped and persists across sessions with no expiration**, while **sessionStorage clears when the page session ends**; both can throw for invalid origins or when persistence is blocked, so reads and writes need `try/catch` and a graceful fallback to URL-only state, and `file://` behavior is undefined, so testing must happen over `http://localhost` ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Window/localStorage)). Prefer an opaque result code over a raw answer vector, since encoded answers leak into browser history and referrers. Hosting a strictly static build is easy on any provider — Cloudflare Pages' free tier allows **20,000 files, 500 builds per month, and 100 custom domains** — but Cloudflare has no documented native SPA rewrite the way its `_redirects` rules provide, and **GitHub Pages offers no documented rewrite at all**, which is a concrete reason to prefer **hash routing (`#/result?r=…`)** if deploying there ([Cloudflare Pages Limits](https://developers.cloudflare.com/pages/platform/limits/); [GitHub Pages](https://docs.github.com/en/pages/getting-started-with-github-pages/what-is-github-pages)). Accessibility is not optional: implement answers as real **`<button>` or radio inputs** with visible focus, meet **WCAG AA contrast of 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text**, keep non-text UI at 3:1, and wrap transitions in `@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce)` ([W3C WAI](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html); [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/Reference/At-rules/@media/prefers-reduced-motion)).

Equally important is what the app must refuse. **Global leaderboards, cross-device streak sync, genuine 1v1 matchmaking, live synchronized games, cash prizes, user-generated quiz banks with moderation, audience-scale live voting, and scheduled push notifications** all require accounts, servers, legal exposure, or content operations, and are out of scope for a static page ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/behind-the-product-duolingo-streaks); [Wikipedia: QuizUp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QuizUp); [Wikipedia: HQ (video game)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HQ_(video_game))). The subtler refusal is social-proof fabrication: a **"you beat X% of players"** claim needs a real population, and in a personality-adjacent quiz where credibility is the whole product, faking it is a trust risk — score against a hardcoded difficulty curve or par score instead, and label it honestly. A streak can also become a hollow "you opened the page" counter once the fixed content bank is exhausted, so it should be tied to **variable outcomes** (a new daily question, an improving score) rather than mere visits; Duolingo's streak only works because a new lesson genuinely exists tomorrow.

## Conclusion

The counterintuitive lesson is the order of operations: the **result card, not the question set, is the product**, so the six to eight flattering archetypes and their share artifacts should be designed before a single question is written — the same "results first" discipline that made the BuzzFeed format reproducible at scale. For a Singaporean food quiz, the theme's social density is itself the distribution strategy: because hawker dishes are a shared cultural vocabulary, the result is already a conversational object, which lowers the cost of forwarding it into a group chat even as platform-mediated reach has collapsed. The biggest risks are not technical but epistemic — a thin quiz that flips results on retake, or a fabricated percentile, undermines the trust that makes the identity claim land. Two open questions deserve verification before launch: the exact current creative specs for Instagram Stories safe zones and for link previews in the chat apps where Singaporeans actually share, neither of which could be confirmed from primary sources in this research. Build the single static page, but treat the result artifact and the honesty of its claims as the engineering problem worth solving.
